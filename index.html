Food inequality

Fish and fruit for the rich, whole milk and chips for the poor

The richest tenth of households spend three times more per person on alcohol and twice as much on fish as the poorest. Official figures show how income shapes what ends up on Britain’s plates.

By Lenny Baremond · Data: DEFRA Family Food 2024–25

Walk down any supermarket aisle and the shelves look the same for everyone. What ends up in the trolley does not. According to the latest Family Food survey published by the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (DEFRA), the gap between Britain’s richest and poorest households shows up in what they buy as well as in how much they spend.

The survey splits households into ten equal groups, or deciles, according to their income adjusted for household size. In 2024–25 people in the poorest tenth spent on average £27.91 a week each on food and soft drinks brought home. Those in the richest tenth spent £40.55. That difference of about £12.60 a week is not spread evenly across the basket.

Where the money goes

The widest gaps are in the little luxuries: top-earning households spend 3.1 times more per person on alcoholic drinks and 2.5 times more on tea and coffee than those at the bottom. The divide also runs through fresh food. The richest tenth spend 2.2 times more on fish and 1.9 times more on fresh fruit and on fresh green vegetables.

At the other end of the scale, the picture flips. The poorest households spend more per person on whole milk, more than twice as much, and slightly more on chips. Spending on soft drinks is almost identical across the income scale, a ratio of just 1.07.

Reading the chart

Each pair of bars shows how many pence one person spends, on average, each week on a food group. The gap shown in the tooltip divides the higher-income figure by the lower-income one. Above 1, the better-off spend more; below 1, the less well-off do. The survey is based on around 5,000 households, so a few categories, such as flour or fresh meat, have a large margin of error.

Spending more does not always mean eating more: wealthier households often pay more for the same product, from organic vegetables to sustainably caught fish. But the same pattern turns up across almost all fresh produce, and that points to a real difference in what people eat.

The meal out that only some can afford

The divide is starkest away from home. In 2024–25 people in the poorest tenth spent £5.19 a week each on food and soft drinks eaten out, in cafés, canteens, takeaways and restaurants. In the richest tenth the figure was £16.99, more than three times as much. Select “Eating out” on the chart below to see how this gap has changed since 2001.

During the 2020–21 lockdowns, spending on eating out collapsed for everyone, and in cash terms neither group has yet returned to its pre-pandemic level. The “At home” view tells a steadier story: since the early 2000s the richest tenth have spent between 1.3 and 1.8 times as much per person as the poorest. Food prices have risen sharply since 2022, and the households at the bottom have the least room to absorb that.

How we did it

Figures come from DEFRA’s Family Food datasets, based on the Living Costs and Food Survey. We used the “equivalised income decile” tables, which rank households by income adjusted for their size. We kept the 24 main food groups to avoid counting the same spending twice. Spending is in pence per person per week, at current prices. DEFRA Family Food datasets (gov.uk)
